Forensic science is not just for the police!

Published 10/20/25 | Geologic Fever | Tunnel Vision Weekly #10

By S. S. Illite

An important topic for an important issue. Welcome to the world of being an Earth scientist!

Much of the Earth sciences rely on forensics and your ability to deduct as much as you can from what is given to you. Geologists have to rely on all of their senses (literally!) to do their most effective identification.

The Mohs hardness scale is a 1—10 scale that helps scientists determine the strength of a material compared to other items. Depending on how you use it, and what you use it to identify, you can discover lots of identifiable information about the rock or mineral you are identifying!

Here is a simple text version of the scale. On the right is the Mohs numerical value, and the middle column compares "everyday items" (let's be real, how many of you are familiar with mica?) to the minerals on the left.
Mineral Everyday Item Hardness
Talc 1
Gypsum 2
Fingernail 2.5
Calcite Mica 3
Copper coin 3.2
Fluorite 4
Apatite Glass 5
Knife blade, nail 5.5
Feldspar 6
Steel file 6.5
Quartz Sandpaper 7
Topaz 8
Corundum 9
Diamond 10

Talc is very soft and often called soapstone. Most diamonds you see are cut or otherwise processed, which gives them their distinct shapes and defined angles. Like many gemstones, diamonds start out rough! These two are harder to tell apart by color, but as any baby Earth scientist learns, color is misleading. Things like strength, reaction to hydrochloric acid (HCl), growth patterns, and streak color are much more defining.

"Streak" has many definitions, and trust me, geologists are not the ones you need to worry about ditching their pants to identify rocks. A streak is a powder residue left over on a streak plate when a rock/mineral is dragged across it. These plates are made out of unglazed porcelain or silica, and can come in black or white. Hardness-wise, they are a 6.5 on the Mohs scale. Sometimes this streak isn't even close to the same color as the actual rock/mineral it came from! So cool!
