Mezzanine Review

Published 4/21/26 | Subterranean Homesick Blues | Tunnel Vision Weekly #18

By π

If there was ever a record that felt like it was made not in a studio but in a bunker, with the walls closing in and the air thinning out, it'd be Mezzanine. Massive Attack have always been architects of mood, but here it feels like they stopped designing homes and started creating monoliths; enormous, alien, and cold. As the story goes, 3D and Daddy G were barely speaking. Mushroom, the group's third pillar, essentially walked out midway through, his contributions either scrapped or buried beneath layers of guitar distortion he never signed off on. What remained was a record forged in toxicity and stubbornness as two men pulled a sound in opposite directions until the tension itself became the point. If Blue Lines was a conversation and Protection was a seduction, Mezzanine is an argument that neither side is willing to lose.

"Angel" opens the record as a perfect tone setter for what's to come. That bassline (you already know the one) swallows the atmosphere in any room that it's played in. You feel it in your molars before you register it as music. Horace Andy's falsetto floats over the top like a spirit caught between planes, too pure for the wreckage underneath him. "Risingson" follows and strips things even further back, 3D half-rapping, half-confessing over a beat that sounds like a migraine set to a metronome. "Teardrop" is, of course, the centerpiece. Elizabeth Fraser's vocal hovers somewhere between a lullaby and a last rite. The harpsichord loop is extremely delicate and adds a reprieve for the moody soundscape. This coupled with the lyrical vulnerability on display makes it easy to see why it's the band's most iconic track.

"Inertia Creeps" in my opinion stands as one of the records most underappreciated tracks. It's a slow crawl where the deranged 3D whispers about surveillance over a beat that shivers and twitches like its laced with amphetamine. "Exchange" and "Dissolved Girl" sit in the album's midsection like sediment, thick and intoxicating, Sara Jay's voice on the latter adding a narcotic haze that makes the whole thing feel like sinking into deep murky water with no hope of resurfacing. "Group Four" and "Black Milk" close the record in a wash of strings and dread, a perfect tonal conclusion to this moody swan song to the group as we knew them. The collaborator list reads like a deliberately curated conflux of artistic minds: Elizabeth Fraser of the Cocteau Twins lending her otherworldly instrument to a record that couldn't be further from Heaven or Las Vegas, Horace Andy returning as the group's spiritual anchor, and a relatively unknown Sara Jay delivering performances that rival either of them. But the secret weapon might be Angelo Badalamenti's orchestral arrangements, the man who scored David Lynch's filmography lending his cinematic unease is the cherry on top for the records brooding atmosphere.

This album doesn't feel like it should work. It's bleak, slow, and hostile. The band was actively disintegrating while they made it and the production is so dense it borders on claustrophobic. Nothing they made before had this weight. Yet it's hard not to feel like the album was made not in spite of domestic conflict but as a child of it. It's tension manifested into sound waves.
