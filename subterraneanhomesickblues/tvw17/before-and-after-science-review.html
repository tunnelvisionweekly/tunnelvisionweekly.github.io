Before and After Science Review

Published 4/9/2026 | Subterranean Homesick Blues | Tunnel Vision Weekly #17

By π

If there was any one person you could point to to resemble the near mythical status of diligence and ingenuity of a Mozart or Bach, it'd be Brian Eno. There's an iconic story about Before and After Science: It goes that Eno recorded nearly 100 tracks for it, then spent 2 years chipping and widdling them down to just ten pictures. Beano was fresh off of producing Bowie's seminal run of Low and Heroes, and had come away with a fetish for perfectionism. Before and After Science is the vindication for his efforts. The record (like its cover) is binary, as it is split neatly into two stylistic halves. Side A is unhinged, jingly, and rhythmic; Side B is moody, ambient, layered, and simply gorgeous. It's as if Eno couldn't decide which polarized character to choose: David Byrn's oddball mentor or postmodernist architect, so he simply did both.

The upbeat opening tracks are haunted with a gleeful paranoia. “No One Receiving” has Eno embodies a Voyager-esque satellite, existentializing about its transmission of data just for it to fall on deaf ears. “Backwater” is a polished diamond of Art pop—it's catchy, kinetic, and has a chorus you'll find yourself humming when you're in the shower. “By this River” pivots the records tempo into a transcendental calm. Its gentle, clinically simple piano backdrops a stanza about stillness and the flow of time. It's delicate and ethereal like the subject its reflecting on. “Through Hollow Lands” and “Spider and I” envelope you in textured electronic orchestration. These proto-ambient pieces swell with hymns and melodies, a sneak peak of where Eno would take us with his ambient series near the end of the '70s.

The liner notes read like a who's who of mid-seventies art-rock royalty: Phil Collins and Percy Jones from Brand X on the rhythmic tracks, Jaki Liebezeit of Can, Fred Frith on guitar, and Robert Fripp lurking somewhere in the background being Robert Fripp. What's remarkable is how all these avant garde idiosyncratic musicians serve the songs rather than splinter them. Eno has a gift for massaging virtuosos to play with restraint and evaporate the ego for the greater artistic tapestry.

This album would be the last of what would be his four “song” albums from the '70s, before his complete departure into the ambient and orchestral work that would come to define the later stage of his staggering career. In this context, this album feels like a swan song from one mode of creation, and an embracement of another. The title itself is an allusion to this threshold. While it may not be held to the same stature as his more iconic records, this stands to me to be his most complete. It takes elements of the paranoid energy from Warm Jets, couples it with the playful absurdism of Tiger Mountain, and finishes it with the sheen and refinement that you'd later find on Music for Airports. The album stands as a perfect balance of pioneering ideas from one of the great musical geniuses of our time.
