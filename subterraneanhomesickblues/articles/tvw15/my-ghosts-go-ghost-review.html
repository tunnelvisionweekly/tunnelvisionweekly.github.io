My Ghosts Go Ghost Review

Published 2/25/26 | Subterranean Homesick Blues | Tunnel Vision Weekly #15

By π

Arizona hip-hop darlings By Storm have delivered an exceptional breakout album that operates like a controlled demolition of grief. By Storm is the phantom that rose from what was left of Injury Reserve, the trio whose founding member Stepa J. Groggs died in 2020 while the group was still drawing its first real breaths. Surviving members RiTchie and producer Parker Corey poured that devastation into Injury Reserve's final transmission, By the Time I Get To Phoenix, a personal favorite of that year, and a record that sounded like it was decomposing in your hands as you listened. They buried the name with Groggs. My Ghosts Go Ghost, released on deadAir Records, is not a sequel to that funeral. It's a reflection of what it means to move on once the casket has been buried.

The album opens with "Can I Have You For Myself?", where RiTchie addresses impending fatherhood over frigid, looping guitar chords and synthetic crooning that circles like a moth trapped against glass, before the whole arrangement implodes without warning. "Dead Weight" and "Zig Zag" descend further into Corey's labyrinth, a grainy electronic crackle giving way to chaotic whiplash breakbeats. "Zig Zag" is the album's cathedral moment, a melancholic acoustic line that metamorphoses over six minutes into a shimmering collage of downsampled drums and glitchy filigree.

The emotional marrow lives in "In My Town," where desolate guitar work channels Talk Talk–era post-rock. RiTchie lays his life flat on the table: DoorDashing between tour stops, writing lyrics between deliveries like prayers scribbled on napkins. Most rappers build mythologies, RiTchie builds an itemized receipt of what it actually costs to make art when nobody's buying. "Double Trio 2" tears the ceiling off, maximalist, explosive, and "And I Dance" is the light that floods through the gap: euphoric, breakbeat-laced, almost recklessly colorful. The closer "GGG" doesn't resolve. A bluesy guitar riff curls through lyrics heavy with the exhaustion of a man who has learned to live alongside his wound. The album doesn't end so much as it stops bleeding.

Parker and RiTchie haven't rebranded, they've performed an autopsy on their old selves and built something new from what they found still beating. My Ghosts Go Ghost wears its stitches on the outside. Nine dark meditations with Corey's most disciplined production to date, and RiTchie rapping like a man who has finally accepted that grief doesn't leave. It just passively haunts you as you continue to live.
