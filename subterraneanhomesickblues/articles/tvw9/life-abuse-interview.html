    Exclusive Interview with Life Abuse

    Published 10/6/25 | Subterranean Homesick Blues | Tunnel Vision Weekly #9

    By π

    Read on for π's exclusive interview with Life Abuse!

    How did Life Abuse come into being, especially given the members' histories in bands like Deathsquad, Under Attack, Male Patterns, etc.? Was there a moment or series of events that made you say "this is the time to do it again"?

    Dan: Everyone in the band has been friends since around 1999. We have all played music together in one form or another. In early 2023, Mark sent me some songs that he had been working on with a few other people. Those people that he had been playing with were no longer able to take part in the band. He asked me if I wanted to play drums on the songs. At that point, we asked Nate if he'd be interested in playing guitar. Sean had recently moved back to the Albany area. I had bumped into him at a record release show for my old band, Male Patterns. Sean had played in one of my favorite local bands, Police Line, and I thought he would be a great fit.

    Mark: Life Abuse came into being in July of 2020. It was a project that I started during COVID. It was me and a few friends who were traveling for work. We said, "Hey, the world is falling apart. Let's write some music on the road to ruin." However by November it sort of went to the backburner since all our main bands were becoming active again. It got shelved until December of 2022. My band (Under Attack) had broken up in October and I was trying to figure out what I wanted to do. My friends' bands were very much active so they didn't have any interest in continuing the project. They encouraged me to do this band again since I had come up with the name and written the songs. I felt like the band was a continuation of DeathSquad so Dan was the first person I thought of and reached out to about playing. Even though it seemed totally crazy to try to take this on I felt like it made total sense. Dan has always been incredible to make music with as are Sean and Nate. We just came together like Voltron.

    Given you're based across different states (NY, VA, maybe others), how do you make collaboration work? What tools/processes help you when you can't all physically be together?

    Sean: We get together about once a month to play and just make good use of our time together. Mark also stays on top of sending us new riffs and tweaks to songs via email, and we have a pretty active group chat going. Plus we all practice regularly on our own. Honestly, we get more done in a few hours a month than bands I've been in that practiced twice a week.

    Mark: Sometimes we go out for Indian food before practice and brainstorm what we're thinking. The songs tend to change a lot from when we start working on them to when they are in final form. Then Dan, Sean or I will say, "Okay, this is the title I'm feeling for this song. I will write the lyrics for this one." We feel it's important that our lyrics and message also represent us as a collective and have meaning.

    How would you describe your sound to someone who hasn't heard Systematization yet—what influences were top of mind (both crustpunk, hardcore, metal, etc.)? Are there specific bands whose sound or approach you tried to draw from or push against?

    Dan: If I had to describe our sound, I would say it is a mix of crust, punk and hardcore punk heavily influenced by Swedish hardcore bands like No Security, G-Anx, Wolf Brigade plus bands like Amon Amarth and At the Gates. Megadeth is also a big influence on the band as well as ABC Diablo and some American bands like Negative Approach, Talk is Poison, and Dropdead.

    You said in your interview with Micheal Thorn that you have had song ideas that were on this album going back over 20 years. Can you talk about specific songs on Systematization that were especially challenging to write or record? Were there ideas or riffs that you fought over or changed a lot before landing on the final form?

    Sean: We never fought over riffs or anything, but we do communicate openly about how we'd like songs to change as we write them. Mark brings in most of the riffs and the basic songs, but we all spend a lot of time arranging, suggesting leads and fills, and so forth. "Reconciliation" and "Life Cycle" were older Deathsquad tunes and differ a lot from the initial practice demos, especially the sweeping intros. "Give Rise" was largely written in practice, too, which is unusual for us. It helps vastly that Dan is a monster drummer—I lock in with him really easily. That being said, sometimes a song just clicks immediately. Overall we put a huge amount of thought into our music, which I couldn't be happier about.

    Dan: Some of the material on this album is reworked versions of Deathsquad songs. Mark, Nate and I played in Deathsquad together back in the early 2000s. The band stopped before we were able to record some of the material we had written. Some of the material on Systematization was written in the summer of 2001. We really liked that stuff and decided to revisit it when Life Abuse started.

    The album was recorded, mixed, and mastered by Will Killingsworth at Dead Air. How did working with him affect the sound? Were there things you couldn't have achieved without that collaboration?

    Sean: Will has always been easy to work with and also understands how we want to sound. He knows hardcore well, doesn't push us in any preconceived direction, but has a good ear for when something is off, and has great feedback when we do ask for it. He's a pleasure to record with.

    How does being part of various scenes (punk, crust, hardcore) shape what you do? Do you feel pressure to "do things a certain way" or expectations from listeners because of your past bands?

    Sean: I pretty much live how I do today because of spending so many years touring in bands and writing music. There's no going back. Punk informed my love for history, it's informed my career choices, it's informed my politics. So for me, the main expectation is to be good—to write smart music; to write smart and interesting lyrics that make people want to dig further into what they're about; to play well. To make sure that I'm actually drawing fully upon those years upon years of experience and putting something good back into this subculture and not to waste its time.

    Mark: We are strong advocates of moving forward and not clinging to past bands we have done. Obviously there are going to be elements and styles from past stuff we have done.

    Dan: We feel no pressure to do anything except write the strongest material we can and put out the best possible records. All of us have been playing in punk bands for a long time. Musical trends come and go. Life Abuse is making the same records we would have made if we had been together back in 2001. We are simply trying to make the music we'd want to listen to.

    What's next for Life Abuse? More touring, splits, experiments in style, perhaps pushing further into other sounds or lyrical themes?

    Dan: We have a split 12" coming out with our Dutch friends Seein' Red and a split 7" with Sweden's Alarm! We also have a few other split records in the works. We have some upcoming shows this fall and winter. We want to try and play out as much as possible.

    Mark: Lyrically, we write from our life experiences. That won't change. Musically, we play the style we have always gravitated towards and probably will never stray too far from that.

    Anything you'd like to share with the great people in the underground?

    Sean: Read. Read about history, read about politics, read fiction, read about art. Inform yourself and ask hard questions. Push back against a world that kills and exploits and divides and funnels money and power upwards to those who already have far, far too much of both. Make sure punk and hardcore mean something. Make art—lots of it. Listen to B.G.K.

    Dan: Subcultures like punk only exist when people make things happen. I encourage anyone reading this who is interested to go learn an instrument, start a band, put out a record, book a show, make a flyer for a show. Feel free to take part.

    Mark: We're living in extremely dark times. It's easy to feel like there is no hope and no point but that's what they're trying to do to us. Now more than ever we need to be there for each other. It's important to keep punk and hardcore as a subculture, a place where people can not only feel safe to be themselves but know and understand that they're accepted as part of a community.
