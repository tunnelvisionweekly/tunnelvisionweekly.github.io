The Epic Review

Published 12/11/25 | Subterranean Homesick Blues | Tunnel Vision Weekly #13

By π

Kamasi Washington's The Epic is not an album that is heard—it's inhabited. At nearly three hours long, it moves with the gravity of something mythic, the kind of project that makes today's streaming optimized culture feel hollow by comparison. Calling it an album feels almost inadequate; The Epic is closer to a personal cosmology, a universe Kamasi meticulously crafted and now serves as its mystical sculler.

The first thing you notice is scale. Not in the bloated "throw it at the wall and see what sticks" way that often sinks longer projects, but in a way that feels architectural. Washington writes like he's designing cathedrals out of sound with grand, towering pieces where every saxophone run, every choral swell, every subtle string flourish is another stained-glass window letting light in at a different angle.

Tracks like "Change of the Guard" explode with cinematic urgency, as if Washington is scoring the first moments of the universe itself. His sax tone is broad, warm, and it weeps and dreams along with us. It acts less like a solo instrument and more like a narrator. Meanwhile, the West Coast Get Down provides a rhythm section with an otherworldly force. Their playing is tight enough to be disciplined, while loose enough to feel alive.

But epic length means nothing without emotional architecture, and that's where Washington separates himself. Across the three discs, he swings between spiritual uplift and existential wandering without losing cohesion. "Re Run Home" recalls the neofunk energy of Herbie Hancock with the precision of Charles Mingus; "The Magnificent Seven" embodies the feeling of hopeful extensionalism, reminding us how small we really are. And then there's the choir, the album's secret weapon. They don't just complement the project they transcend. Their presence turns Washington's compositions into something liturgical, sonic scapes so remarkable they can bring life to the dead.

What makes The Epic land especially hard today, in a world of constant bombardment and stress, is how confidently it slows the world down. Washington rejects the idea that complexity is alienating. Instead, he makes it inviting. His jazz isn't homework. It's a gentle journey in a world of his making. You don't have to "understand" it in any academic sense; you just have to let its scale calibrate your sense of what modern music is allowed to be.

Yes, it's long. Yes, it demands patience. But the reward is rare: an album that expands and folds and grows in your mind for years. Nothing feels accidental, nothing feels disposable, and nothing feels small. The Epic reminds you that scale can be an artist's greatest tool when it's backed by talent and intention, and that sometimes the most radical thing an artist can do is refuse to shrink.
