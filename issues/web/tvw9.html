    Finds of the Week: TV/PC Combo, Videotape Reels

    The find of the week this week was a find that surprised me at how pristine it looked for being in the tech dump. It was a 2000s Sony TV/PC combo, with the feature ad stickers still on the screen! The screen was immaculate, and it all turned on. All that it needed was some ram and a HDD, and you could have gotten yourself a working (DDR2 RAM) PC w/ a Blu-ray player! Neat find, it's not often you come across something like this.

    A close second this time around was a set of magnetic tape reels, the master copies for some of the VHS's you could see in the DCC. The reels are heavy, metal circles, and some of the reels have over 3000 feet of tape in them! Only issue is, how do you play them? They've got what looks like ¾" or even 1" tape, which is way thicker than a VHS tape. You can most likely have a look for yourself in the DCC if you wanna see 'em!

    Geologic Fever

    There's no medicine to save you from geology!

    —By S. S. Illite

    Little things count just as much as the massive. Geology is a crazy but awfully dynamic argument for size not mattering nearly as much.

    Geologically speaking, we are brand new. Babies. Not in a demeaning manner, but in relating to the sheer magnitude of the geologic timescale, the National Park Service put it simply:

    "This analogy highlights the relative length of each geologic time period. Spread your arms wide. With the span of your arms representing all geologic time, look at one hand; your fingertips represent the formation of Earth and the beginning of geologic time."

    It is practically unfathomable to perceive something so comparably miniscule. We are maybe a fleck of skin, if not a proton of an atom of a cell. We rely so heavily on our own perceptions to move through life, yet how do we move through life trying to perceive the practically imperceivable?

    We study. We conduct experiments, we take notes, we teach, we observe, and we speak out for what's important. Geology is a vital science, and it being such an underrepresented science can be detrimental to the efforts of our scientists. Vitality commands all efforts, no matter how big or small; when vitality is questioned, efforts tend to cease.

    Representation is important, and that's how we share knowledge. And that's the goal of this column! To teach and develop skills with the community is a privilege.

    Subterranean Homesick Blues

    —π

    Explosive and unapologetic, Charli XCX's Vroom Vroom, released in 2016, is not just an EP, it's a prophecy. Across its brief 4 song tracklist, Charli tears away from her mainstream (and honestly dull) pop image and launches herself into the bombastic hyperpop soaked sound that she would come to perfect. Produced primarily by SOPHIE, the late great visionary behind some of the most forward-thinking electronic music of the 2010s, Vroom Vroom is abrasive, metallic, and unapologetically synthetic. It represents a shift not only in Charli's career, but for pop music as a whole marking the moment where the underground PC Music aesthetic collided with the mainstream and redeﬁned what "pop music" could sound like.

    The opening and title track, "Vroom Vroom," sets the tone immediately: robotic bass lines, distorted percussion, and Charli's deadpan delivery combine into a sound that feels like it's been beamed from a neon-lit, cyberpunk dream. "Let's ride," she commands, turning a car into a metaphor for control, power, and self-possession. The lyrics are simple, even absurd at times, but don't let that fool you. Charli's icy conﬁdence and tongue-in-cheek vocals make it clear she knows exactly what she's doing. It's ironic, it's aggressive, and it's addictive.

    "Trophy" continues the adrenaline rush, with glitchy punchy drums and sharp-edged synths that sound like a digital engine revving. SOPHIE's ﬁngerprints are unmistakable here as the track shifts and mutates constantly, blending sweetness and chaos into something that feels mechanical yet danceable. Charli sounds like she's performing inside a titanium spaceship as her chipmunked vocals soar over the anodic snares and prominent vocal samples from Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill.

    "Paradise" offers a rare glimpse of warmth, featuring Hannah Diamond's breathy vocals over a glossy, dreamlike beat that describes love in the digital metascape. It is synthetic, ﬂeeting, and strangely innocent.

    The closing track, "Secret (Shh)," wraps things up with whispery vocals and rubbery baselines that feel both playful and sinister. Over synths blaring like perverse sirens, Charli tells us how she is ready to lose control and that she will only tell us how far she will go if we promise to keep her secret. It's seductive and sleek while still complimenting the baleful energy of SOPHIE's production.

    What makes Vroom Vroom so compelling is how boldly it rejects convention. It's not radio-friendly nor approachable and it's not meant to be. Instead, it embraces artiﬁciality, noise, and synthetic excess to form a beautiful glitchy cacophony. The reception of this EP upon release was mixed, it received critical praise amongst PC Music fans, but many Angles found themselves unsure of what to make of her new sound. Fast-forward to today and this album has served as a launching point for the explosion of hyperpop music that followed.

    In just sixteen minutes, Charli XCX dismantled her own cliche pop star image and rebuilt it from silicon and steel. Vroom Vroom serves as the sound of pop's future arriving early and unapologetically. It remains one of the most important and thrilling releases of her career, a perfect collision of bubblegum and chaos that still sounds ahead of its time.

    Bonus: Sneak Peek at π's interview with Life Abuse

    How did Life Abuse come into being, especially given the members' histories in bands like Deathsquad, Under Attack, Male Patterns, etc.? Was there a moment or series of events that made you say "this is the time to do it again"?

    Dan: Everyone in the band has been friends since around 1999. We have all played music together in one form or another. In early 2023, Mark sent me some songs that he had been working on with a few other people. Those people that he had been playing with were no longer able to take part in the band. He asked me if I wanted to play drums on the songs. At that point, we asked Nate if he'd be interested in playing guitar. Sean had recently moved back to the Albany area. I had bumped into him at a record release show for my old band, Male Patterns. Sean had played in one of my favorite local bands, Police Line, and I thought he would be a great fit.

    Mark: Life Abuse came into being in July of 2020. It was a project that I started during COVID. It was me and a few friends who were traveling for work. We said, "Hey, the world is falling apart. Let's write some music on the road to ruin." However by November it sort of went to the backburner since all our main bands were becoming active again. It got shelved until December of 2022. My band (Under Attack) had broken up in October and I was trying to figure out what I wanted to do. My friends' bands were very much active so they didn't have any interest in continuing the project. They encouraged me to do this band again since I had come up with the name and written the songs. I felt like the band was a continuation of DeathSquad so Dan was the first person I thought of and reached out to about playing. Even though it seemed totally crazy to try to take this on I felt like it made total sense. Dan has always been incredible to make music with as are Sean and Nate. We just came together like Voltron.

    How does being part of various scenes (punk, crust, hardcore) shape what you do? Do you feel pressure to "do things a certain way" or expectations from listeners because of your past bands?

    Sean: I pretty much live how I do today because of spending so many years touring in bands and writing music. There's no going back. Punk informed my love for history, it's informed my career choices, it's informed my politics. So for me, the main expectation is to be good—to write smart music; to write smart and interesting lyrics that make people want to dig further into what they're about; to play well. To make sure that I'm actually drawing fully upon those years upon years of experience and putting something good back into this subculture and not to waste its time.

    Mark: We are strong advocates of moving forward and not clinging to past bands we have done. Obviously there are going to be elements and styles from past stuff we have done.

    Dan: We feel no pressure to do anything except write the strongest material we can and put out the best possible records. All of us have been playing in punk bands for a long time. Musical trends come and go. Life Abuse is making the same records we would have made if we had been together back in 2001. We are simply trying to make the music we'd want to listen to.
