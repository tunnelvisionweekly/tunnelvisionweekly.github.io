    Tunnel Vision: Out of the Tunnel

    The Greene Building

    —By West Virginia

    Chances are if you're not an architecture student you haven't spent much time here. Don't get too thirsty while inside, they have very few water fountains. A quick sweep of the Greene building left me coming up empty waterbottled (if you'll allow me the wordplay).

    Continuing my search, I went to the office on the main floor to see if someone could direct me to a source of water. I was directed to the staff kitchen across the hall where I was greeted with a water bubbler (Left). The bubbler itself dispensed chilled filtered water with an option to dispense hot water as well. An ideal situation for water bottle refilling, but not for those who are just looking for a quick sip of water. Its unique location within the staff kitchen also means that it resides behind doors that remain locked once the office closes on weekday evenings and all weekend. I'm only mentioning this dispenser to inform you of its existence, but know that it will be excluded from our official consideration of the best water fountain.

    As for the relevant fountains in the building, you're left to choose between two options. The front entrance to the Greene building opens to the landing between the first and the main (second) floor. If you head downstairs to the first floor, you'll find the first real water fountain (Right) a short way down the hall to your left. This fountain earned a "Poor" on the temperature scale and barely earned an "Acceptable" on water pressure and water bottle compatibility. Location and Flavor both earned an "Outstanding."

    Journeying upstairs to the fourth floor, on your left you'll find the only other water fountain in the building. While it resides unobstructed along the hallway on your left (while facing away from the main stairwell), its outdated design and muted color makes it easy to miss entirely. Earning an outstanding in both temperature and flavor, a good in water pressure and location and acceptable on water bottle compatibility. This was the winning fountain (Middle).

    Subterranean Homesick Blues

    —by π

    Sickboyrari's City of Doves unfolds like a fog-drenched dream drifting through empty streets at dawn. Its production is beautiful, bleak, and heavy with unspoken weight. The record is woozy, almost understated sometimes. Atmosphere, leaning into ghostly synths, muted trap percussion, and melodies that ebb in and out rather than being tied down by a structured chorus.

    Rari's voice is the anchor and the apparition at once. Slurred, auto-tuned, and emotionally distant, it glides across the production with a sense of detachment with intentional carelessness. His drugged-out delivery makes his perspective feel less like a solid recollection but a dreary hypnogenic vestige of his memories. His delivery never forces attention; it invites the listener inward, into a private world of loss, paranoia, and spiritual unrest.

    The album's themes orbit loneliness, addiction, faith, and the hollowness of material success. Designer labels, street mythology, angels, and demons blur together into a single language of excess and regret. Rari catalogs his pain, painting with texture over sputtering high hats each fading memory of his life of excess. There's a quiet desperation beneath the surface, a sense that every track serves more as meditation rather than a proclamation.

    Production throughout City of Doves remains restrained and immersive. Beats pulse softly, often dissolving before they fully assert themselves, creating a floating sensation that mirrors dissociation. The music rarely peaks; instead, it drifts, allowing sadness and introspection to stretch out and linger. The album is icy cold, the space between Rari and his production reflects the dissonance between Rari and his environment.

    By the end, City of Doves, like a lot of the best hip-hop projects, entrances you into Rari's state of mind. It captures the ache of existing between worlds: fame and anonymity, faith and nihilism, numbness and excess. A pioneer in his sound with a project that serves as a Rosetta Stone for trap music of the last decade.

    Extra: What happened to the old building by the Garage?

    —by I

    At what point does a building deserve to be acknowledged? This is the question I found myself asking after the building that once stood on 95 College Avenue was torn down over break. It was the building by the RPI parking garage, all ornate and old-looking. Turns out it was very close to being the oldest building on campus! I would not be surprised if you haven't seen it though, it was a pretty small building. Not to mention the fact that RPI has no mention of this building anywhere on its websites or campus maps.

    This building, according to the Times Union, stood for around 111 years, standing on RPI campus for at least 50–70. Built initially for a businessman's estate in the late 1800s/early 1900s by architect Alexander Jackson Davis, it was relocated off of the site of where Prospect Park is now sometime during the park's creation.

    Around November 2025, an orange plastic construction fence was placed around the structure, as it had been condemned due to structural failures. They were pretty visible, a side wall was pretty heavily bowing out. Then, over winter break, it was completely torn down, leaving only dead grass in its wake. What's unfortunate is preservationists in Troy had been trying to save the building for some time now, with RPI doing nothing to fix up the building. By the time it was condemned, it was reported that it would cost over $1,000,000 to fix back up.

    On a post on the r/RPI subreddit, people shared their dismay of the demolition, one saying they were "heartbroken," with others also voicing their unhappiness over this change. Some speculated what happened to it, with a leading theory being the RPI nuclear engineers had a bit of an oopsie. Another theory is that someone ate it. Who knows for sure? It happened over break, after all.

    The demolition of the small gatehouse on 95 College Avenue was done without much mention of its impending removal, the final nail in the coffin for a building that sat unacknowledged for decades. It's a landmark that probably thousands of students have seen on their way to classes or cars, and yet RPI hasn't made any mention of its removal. At least an email could have been nice.
